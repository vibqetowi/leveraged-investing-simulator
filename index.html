What Would the Perfect Wealth Strategy Look Like?

An ideal long-term wealth accumulation strategy prioritizes four criteria:

  • Simplicity: No market timing, no stock picking, no financial degree. A mechanical rule executable for 30 years without active management.
  • Recoverable Risk: Downturns occur. The strategy must be maintainable through good and bad market cycles.
  • Excess Returns: Consistent outperformance of a benchmark strategy over long periods.
  • Efficiency: Minimized taxes and fees.

Liquidity is not a primary concern for wealth accumulation. Savings accounts serve that purpose.

Everything that follows depends on one assumption: broad market indexes trend upward over multi-decade periods. This is not a guarantee, but has held for nearly a century.

The Wealth Building Benchmark: Dollar Cost Averaging

Dollar Cost Averaging (DCA) involves investing a fixed amount from each paycheck into a security, most commonly a market index fund. It is straightforward and safe, but contains a structural inefficiency: most capital is deployed late in the accumulation period, when the portfolio is largest. Late crashes dominate outcomes because dollar-weighted exposure concentrates at the end of the timeline.

A margin account allows borrowing against securities holdings. Leverage front-loads market exposure, giving capital more time to compound, thus shifting time risk and amplifying potential returns. Losses are also amplified. If debt gets too high relative to portfolio value, the lender forces liquidation (margin call), often leaving the investor with less than they initially deposited.

Loan-to-Value (LTV) and Leverage Ratio

Loan-to-Value (LTV) is debt as a percentage of total portfolio value. $40K equity, $60K borrowed, $100K portfolio: 60% LTV.

LTV = Debt ÷ Total Portfolio Value × 100

Leverage ratio expresses the same position differently: total position divided by equity. A 60% LTV equals a 2.5x leverage ratio ($100K ÷ $40K).

The simulator uses LTV throughout because the inputs and outputs are percentages.

Outputs:

  • Survival probability: Percentage of scenarios ending strictly above total real deposits.
  • Wealth distribution: For surviving scenarios, final wealth compared to plain DCA with the same monthly budget.
  • Outcome Probability Distribution:

    Every simulation classifies into one of three mutually exclusive categories at the end of the simulation period:

    • Ruin: Ended with real net worth below total real deposits (lost money).
    • Sucker: Ended strictly above total real deposits but underperformed DCA.
    • Profit: Ended strictly above total real deposits and outperformed the DCA baseline in real terms.

How This Calculator Works

This tool uses Monte Carlo simulation to test leveraged investing strategies across randomized market scenarios per strategy.

Step 1: Define Initial Conditions

At the start, the simulator deposits initial capital and borrows up to target LTV ratio. This establishes a starting position: securities (invested portion) and debt.

Step 2: Test Multiple Target LTV Strategies

The simulator tests strategies ranging from DCA (no leverage) to maximum LTV. Each represents a different target ratio: 0% (DCA baseline) through maximal leverage.

Step 3: Run Market Scenarios

For each LTV strategy, the simulator runs randomized market scenarios. For the baseline (DCA with no borrowing), scenarios run for higher statistical precision.

Each simulation runs a sequence of monthly steps. The model is flexible about the market movement generator, but the account logic remains the same:

  1. Run market movements: Generate the monthly return or shock process for the securities portion of the portfolio. The simulator can swap in different market models without changing the rest of the logic.
  2. Interest accrual: Debt grows by the monthly cost of borrowing.
  3. Check margin: If LTV exceeds the threshold, the lender forces a liquidation to restore the margin requirement.
  4. Deposit: The monthly budget pays down debt directly.
  5. LTV restore: If LTV falls below target, the model borrows and buys securities to restore the target ratio.

Step 4: Calculate Real Wealth (Inflation-Adjusted)

For each scenario, final net worth (securities − debt) converts to inflation-adjusted dollars using the specified inflation rate. All wealth values display in today's purchasing power.

Real Wealth = Nominal Net Worth / (1 + Inflation Rate)^Years

Standard Mode Assumptions:

Standard Mode uses research-backed defaults biased toward risk reduction:

  • Borrowing Rate: — Prime rate plus lender spread equals annual cost. Higher than early-2026 rates to account for potential rate increases over -year period.
  • Market Growth: per year — S&P 500 historical average 1950-2024 with dividend reinvestment.
  • Volatility: per year — S&P 500 standard deviation. Annual returns distribute around the mean with this volatility, capturing observed market cycle behavior.
  • Margin Call Threshold: LTV — This is what Wealthsimple uses.
  • Inflation: per year — Long-term U.S. average. All final wealth converts to today's purchasing power using this rate.
  • Black Swan Frequency: 1/96 per month — Approximates the expectation of a 15-30% drop every 8 years.
  • Cash Earnings: 0% — Idle cash earns no return. Conservative assumption that overstates opportunity cost and allows reduction of state variables.

These parameters derive from long-term historical data. Custom Mode permits parameter adjustment based on specific lending terms or market assumptions.

Model Limitations

This calculator models idealized conditions. Real-world factors not modeled:

  • Taxes: Capital gains taxes, dividend taxes, interest deductibility not modeled. Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction and can significantly impact returns.
  • Fees & Transaction Costs: Management fees, trading commissions, bid-ask spreads excluded.
  • Behavioral Factors: Panic-selling during downturns, inability to maintain deposits during job loss, psychological stress not modeled.
  • No Voluntary Sales: Simulation never adjusts strategy or sells voluntarily. Real investors would deleverage proactively, which would reduce ruin probability.

Technical Notes

The model keeps a small but general state vector: current portfolio value, outstanding debt, target LTV, borrowing cost, inflation, expected return (drift), and volatility. These are the current state variables for the simulation, and they may evolve over time depending on the model.

A GBM implementation may keep them fixed for a period, while richer models can let drift, volatility, inflation, or borrowing conditions vary by regime or time. The account mechanics remain the same; only the process generating the market and financing environment changes.

Economic Theory & Model Variants

Leverage & The Spread: Leveraged investing depends on positive carry: borrowing at to invest at captures the spread on borrowed capital. The spread accrues only when markets rise; in declines, debt costs while asset value falls.
Monte Carlo Simulation: Run thousands of randomized scenarios to estimate the probability distribution of outcomes. This lets the calculator compare the odds of ruin, underperformance, and outperformance under a range of borrowing and market conditions.
Geometric Brownian Motion: A classic stochastic process for market returns: random but with drift and volatility. It is one possible market oscillator, and the simulator is structured so other generators can replace it without changing the debt, margin, deposit, or LTV mechanics.
Merton Jump-Diffusion: Adds occasional discontinuous jumps to a base diffusion process, helping represent severe but infrequent market shocks. This is one example of a heavy-tailed return model that may be used in place of a smoother market generator.
GARCH: A volatility-clustering model where shocks affect future variance. This is a useful alternative when the market oscillator should capture changing volatility rather than a fixed annualized assumption.
Markov Chains: A regime-based approach where the market moves between states like calm, stressed, and crisis. These models are helpful when the return process itself depends on macro regime rather than a single diffusion process.
Margin Calls & Forced Liquidation: Lenders enforce maximum Loan-to-Value ratios. Breaching the ratio triggers forced liquidation: positions are sold, debt is repaid from proceeds, and the simulation continues. Standard Mode uses LTV; lender thresholds range from roughly 40-50% (conservative) to 75-80% (aggressive).
Real vs. Nominal Returns: Future dollars have different purchasing power than current dollars. This calculator converts all results to current purchasing power using the specified inflation rate. See also Khan Academy for a plain-language explanation.
Lifecycle Investing: Ayres & Nalebuff's paper is a classic argument for using leverage earlier in life when you have more time and human capital to absorb drawdowns.
